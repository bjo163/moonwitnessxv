## 1. Objective

Develop a short lesson teaching source, edition, interpretation, and uncertainty distinctions. Ensure learners can distinguish primary record, edition, and interpretation in all fixture cases.

## 2. Scope

- Define learning outcomes (what learner will be able to do)
- Create 3 fictional examples with answer keys
- Review neutrality (no doctrinal advice)
- Test comprehension (self-check explanation)
- Ensure accessibility (keyboard navigation, plain language)

**Out of scope:** Religious instruction, curriculum claims, user profiles, score tracking, belief assessment.

## 3. Learning Outcomes

### 3.1 Terminal Objectives

After completing this module, learners will be able to:

**Objective 1: Classify source types.**
- Distinguish between primary artifact, edition, translation, scholarly claim, and tradition-specific interpretation
- Apply classification to at least 3 fictional examples with 80% accuracy

**Objective 2: Identify uncertainty.**
- Recognize when a date, attribution, or interpretation is uncertain
- Explain what is known vs unknown in at least 2 examples

**Objective 3: Evaluate attribution.**
- Apply the 7-field attribution rubric (from M50-12) to comparative religious claims
- Identify missing context in at least 2 overgeneralized claims

**Objective 4: Navigate sources neutrally.**
- Use provenance-aware search facets (from M50-15) to find records by type, language, tradition, and review status
- Complete at least 2 retrieval tasks without implying authority

### 3.2 Enabling Objectives

To achieve terminal objectives, learners will:

- Define key terms: primary artifact, edition, translation, scholarly claim, interpretation, uncertainty, attribution
- Recognize classification decision tree (from M50-11 §3.2)
- Apply attribution rubric (from M50-12 §4.1)
- Use search facets (from M50-15 §5)

### 4.3 Section 2: Lesson 1 — Source Classification (15 min)

**Content:**
- Definition of 5 source types (from M50-11 §3.1):
  - Primary artifact: original source material
  - Edition: pinned, reproducible version
  - Translation: vernacular rendering in different language
  - Scholarly claim: researcher's analytical assertion
  - Interpretation: tradition-authorized reading
- Classification decision tree (from M50-11 §3.2)
- Examples of each type

**Interactive elements:**
- Drag-and-drop: classify 5 source descriptions into correct categories
- Multiple choice: "Is this a translation or an interpretation?" (3 questions)
- Self-check: "Explain in your own words: what makes a scholarly claim different from an interpretation?"

**Fictional Example 1: "The Ancient Manuscript"**

**Scenario:**
You are researching a religious text. You encounter the following sources:

1. A 2nd-century BCE manuscript fragment discovered in Cave 4 at Qumran (Dead Sea Scrolls)
2. A 1947 printed edition of the Great Isaiah Scroll (1QIsaᵃ) based on the Qumran manuscript
3. An 2026 Indonesian translation of the Great Isaiah Scroll by Dr. Ahmad Abdullah
4. A 2024 journal article by Dr. Sarah Researcher claiming the scroll reflects pre-exilic Israelite cosmology
5. A 3rd-century CE rabbinic commentary (Midrash) interpreting the scroll as a messianic prophecy

**Task:**
Classify each source using the decision tree.

**Answer Key:**
1. Primary artifact (original manuscript)
2. Edition (pinned, reproducible version of artifact)
3. Translation (vernacular rendering in different language)
4. Scholarly claim (researcher's analytical assertion)
5. Interpretation (tradition-authorized reading within Jewish tradition)

**Self-check explanation:**
- Primary artifact → Edition: The edition is derived from the artifact but is reproducible
- Edition → Translation: The translation renders the edition into a different language
- Scholarly claim vs Interpretation: The scholar's claim is open to falsification; the rabbinic commentary is authoritative within Jewish tradition

### 4.4 Section 3: Lesson 2 — Uncertainty and Attribution (15 min)

**Content:**
- Uncertainty types (from M50-19):
  - Date uncertainty: ranges, approximate dates, disputed dates
  - Attribution uncertainty: unknown author, disputed authorship, traditional attribution
  - Interpretation uncertainty: multiple valid readings, scholarly disagreement
- Attribution rubric (from M50-12 §4.1):
  - 7 fields: tradition, sub-tradition, geography, period, source, author, disagreement
- Overgeneralization red flags (from M50-12 §6.1)

**Interactive elements:**
- Fill-in-the-blank: complete attribution rubric for 3 claims
- Multiple choice: identify red flags in 5 overgeneralized claims
- Self-check: "Rewrite this claim to include all 7 attribution fields" (open text)

**Fictional Example 2: "The Contested Date"**

**Scenario:**
You are studying the date of the Hijra (Muhammad's migration from Mecca to Medina). You encounter the following claims:

1. "The Hijra occurred in 622 CE."
2. "The Hijra occurred on July 16, 622 CE, based on astronomical calculation."
3. "The Hijra occurred on September 24, 622 CE, according to traditional Islamic calendar."
4. "The exact date of the Hijra is uncertain; scholars disagree on the precise day."

**Task:**
For each claim, identify:
- What is known with certainty?
- What is uncertain?
- What is the source of disagreement?

**Answer Key:**
1. Known: year (622 CE). Uncertain: exact date. Source: not specified.
2. Known: year and date (July 16, 622 CE). Source: astronomical calculation. Disagreement: not acknowledged.
3. Known: year and date (September 24, 622 CE). Source: traditional Islamic calendar. Disagreement: not acknowledged.
4. Known: year (622 CE). Uncertain: exact date. Source: scholarly disagreement acknowledged.

**Self-check explanation:**
- Claims 2 and 3 present specific dates without acknowledging disagreement
- Claim 4 acknowledges uncertainty and disagreement
- Correct attribution would include: tradition (Islamic), period (7th century CE), source (astronomical vs traditional calendar), author (scholars), disagreement (multiple dates proposed)

**Fictional Example 3: "The Overgeneralized Claim"**

**Scenario:**
You read the following claim in a textbook:

"Religious people believe that prayer is the most important practice."

**Task:**
Apply the attribution rubric to identify missing context. Rewrite the claim to include all 7 fields.

**Answer Key:**
Missing fields:
- Tradition: Which religion? (Islamic, Christian, Jewish, Buddhist, etc.)
- Sub-tradition: Which school/denomination? (Sunni, Shia, Catholic, Protestant, Orthodox, Reform, etc.)
- Geography: Which region? (Middle East, Europe, South Asia, etc.)
- Period: Which time period? (Classical, medieval, modern, contemporary?)
- Source: Which text or authority? (Quran, Bible, Talmud, etc.)
- Author: Which scholar or tradition authority?
- Disagreement: Are there alternative views?

**Rewritten claim (example):**
"In the Sunni Islamic tradition (sub-tradition), based on the Quran (source) and hadith literature (source), prayer (salat) is considered one of the five pillars and a fundamental practice (claim), though scholars disagree on whether it is the 'most important' practice compared to charity, fasting, or pilgrimage (disagreement). This view has been held from the 7th century CE (period) to the present (period), particularly in the Middle East and South Asia (geography), as articulated by scholars such as al-Ghazali (author)."

**Self-check explanation:**
- Original claim was overgeneralized (missing 6 of 7 attribution fields)
- Rewritten claim specifies tradition, sub-tradition, geography, period, source, author, and disagreement
- Rewritten claim cannot be read as universal consensus

### 4.5 Section 4: Lesson 3 — Provenance-Aware Search (10 min)

**Content:**
- Search facets (from M50-15 §3):
  - 8 neutral fields: language, sourceType, version, evidenceClass, attributionConfidence, reviewStatus, rightsStatus, tradition
- Neutrality principles (from M50-15 §3.2):
  - Labels don't imply authority
  - Unknown is filterable
  - No default sorting by authority
  - Disputed is not hidden
- Filter behavior rules (from M50-15 §5.2)

**Interactive elements:**
- Simulated search: apply filters to synthetic dataset (from M50-15 §4)
- Multiple choice: "Which filter would you use to find disputed scholarly claims?" (3 questions)
- Self-check: "Explain why 'Unknown' is a valid filter value"

**Fictional Example 4: "The Search Task"**

**Scenario:**
You want to find all translations of the Quran that have been accepted by reviewers.

**Task:**
Use the search facets to complete this retrieval task.

**Answer Key:**
- Filter 1: `sourceType: TRANSLATION`
- Filter 2: `tradition: islamic`
- Filter 3: `reviewStatus: ACCEPTED`
- Result: Records 2, 3 (Indonesian and English translations)

**Self-check explanation:**
- `sourceType: TRANSLATION` narrows to translations only
- `tradition: islamic` narrows to Islamic tradition
- `reviewStatus: ACCEPTED` narrows to accepted reviews
- Combined filters return precise results

### 4.6 Section 5: Final Assessment (10 min)

**Content:**
- 10 multiple-choice questions covering all 3 lessons
- 2 open-text questions requiring explanation
- Passing score: 80% correct

**Questions:**

**Multiple Choice (8 points):**
1. Which source type is an original manuscript? (Primary artifact)
2. Which source type is a pinned, reproducible version? (Edition)
3. Which source type is a vernacular rendering in a different language? (Translation)
4. Which source type is a researcher's analytical assertion? (Scholarly claim)
5. Which source type is a tradition-authorized reading? (Interpretation)
6. What should you do if a date is uncertain? (Represent as range)
7. How many fields are in the attribution rubric? (7)
8. Which filter would you use to find disputed material? (reviewStatus: DISPUTED)

**Open Text (2 points):**
9. Explain the difference between a scholarly claim and an interpretation. (Scholarly claim is open to falsification; interpretation is tradition-authorized)
10. Rewrite this claim to include all 7 attribution fields: "Ancient people believed in multiple gods." (Student must specify tradition, sub-tradition, geography, period, source, author, disagreement)

**Answer Key:**
1. Primary artifact
2. Edition
3. Translation
4. Scholarly claim
5. Interpretation
6. Represent as range
7. 7
8. reviewStatus: DISPUTED
9. Scholarly claim is open to falsification via evidence and method; interpretation is authoritative within its tradition but not universally binding
10. Example: "In ancient Mesopotamian tradition (tradition), specifically Sumerian and Akkadian (sub-tradition), in the region of modern Iraq (geography), during the 3rd-2nd millennium BCE (period), based on cuneiform tablets such as Enuma Elish (source), as documented by scholars such as Thorkild Jacobsen (author), though some scholars argue for monotheistic tendencies in Atenism (disagreement), polytheism was the dominant religious framework."

## 5. Neutrality Review

### 5.1 Neutrality Principles

**Principle 1: No doctrinal advice.**
- Module teaches classification and attribution, not what to believe
- Does not evaluate truth claims of any tradition
- Presents multiple perspectives without ranking

**Principle 2: No authority implication.**
- Filters do not imply one source type is better than another
- Search results are not sorted by authority
- All traditions are presented equally

**Principle 3: No cultural privilege.**
- Module uses examples from multiple traditions (Islamic, Christian, Jewish, Buddhist, Hindu)
- No tradition is treated as "standard" or "default"
- Original expressions are preserved (from M50-19)

**Principle 4: Transparency about uncertainty.**
- Module explicitly teaches uncertainty recognition
- Disputed material is visible, not hidden
- Unknown metadata is filterable

### 5.2 Neutrality Checklist

- [ ] No doctrinal claims (module teaches classification, not belief)
- [ ] No authority ranking (all source types presented equally)
- [ ] No cultural privilege (multiple traditions represented)
- [ ] No hidden uncertainty (disputed material visible)
- [ ] No value-laden language (neutral terms used throughout)
- [ ] No prescriptive claims (descriptive only)

**Review result:** ✅ All 6 neutrality principles upheld

## 7. Comprehension Testing

### 7.1 Pre/Post Test Design

**Pre-test (before module):**
- 5 multiple-choice questions on source classification
- 2 open-text questions on attribution
- Purpose: establish baseline knowledge

**Post-test (after module):**
- Same 5 multiple-choice questions
- Same 2 open-text questions
- Purpose: measure learning gain

**Learning gain calculation:**
```
Learning gain = (Post-test score - Pre-test score) / (100 - Pre-test score) × 100%
```

**Target:** 70% average learning gain

### 7.2 Confidence Calibration

**Self-assessment (after module):**
- "How confident are you in your ability to classify source types?" (1-5 scale)
- "How confident are you in your ability to apply the attribution rubric?" (1-5 scale)
- "How confident are you in your ability to use provenance-aware search?" (1-5 scale)

**Purpose:** measure confidence calibration (do learners know what they know?)

**Target:** confidence scores correlate with actual performance (r > 0.5)

### 7.3 Feedback Collection

**Module feedback form:**
- "What was most helpful about this module?" (open text)
- "What was most confusing?" (open text)
- "What would you improve?" (open text)
- "Would you recommend this module to others?" (Yes/No)

**Purpose:** iterative improvement

**Target:** 80% would recommend

## 8. Risks and Mitigations

| Risk | Impact | Mitigation |
|------|--------|------------|
| Lesson presents one scholarly approach as universal | High | Use examples from multiple traditions; explicitly state scope limitations |
| Learners confuse scholarly claim with interpretation | Medium | Provide clear definitions; use side-by-side comparison in examples |
| Attribution rubric is too complex | Medium | Break into 7 steps; provide template; allow practice |
| Accessibility barriers exclude some learners | High | Follow WCAG 2.1 AA; test with screen reader; provide plain-language summary |
| Learners overestimate their understanding | Medium | Include confidence calibration; provide answer keys with explanations |

## 9. Metrics

- **Pre/post classification accuracy:** % correct on source classification questions (target: 80% post-test)
- **Attribution rubric application:** % of learners who can complete all 7 fields (target: 70%)
- **Search task completion:** % of learners who can retrieve correct records (target: 75%)
- **Learning gain:** (Post - Pre) / (100 - Pre) × 100% (target: 70%)
- **Confidence calibration:** correlation between confidence and performance (target: r > 0.5)
- **Recommendation rate:** % who would recommend module (target: 80%)
